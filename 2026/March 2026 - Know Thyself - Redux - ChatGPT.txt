On the wall of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, just two words were carved—words that have challenged humanity for centuries: **“Know thyself.”**

The Greek philosopher Socrates, who lived more than two thousand years ago, passionately believed in this idea.

One day, Socrates was walking through a busy marketplace, lost in deep thought. Distracted, he accidentally bumped into someone. Annoyed, the man snapped, “Can’t you see where you’re going? Who are you?”

Socrates smiled and replied, “My dear friend, I’ve been trying to figure that out for many years. Please let me know if you discover the answer.”

Socrates later faced trial and was condemned to death. He was accused of disobeying the state and corrupting the youth with his teachings. Before his death, he made a remarkable statement: **“The unexamined life is not worth living.”**

That statement made me pause and ask a simple question: **Who am I?**

Am I someone from India?
Am I a software engineer?
Am I simply a middle-aged man?

Recently, I looked at a photo of myself from my school days. I looked completely different—almost unrecognizable. And it struck me that twenty years from now, I will probably look just as different again.

So if my appearance changes, my roles change, and even my life circumstances change… then who exactly am I?

In the ancient scriptures of India, it is said that our physical body is made of five elements: earth, fire, water, air, and space.

Our flesh and bones eventually return to the earth.
Our bodies carry warmth, symbolizing fire—the same energy that allows us to digest food.
Blood flows through our veins like water.
We breathe air every moment, without which life cannot continue.
And within our bodies are hollow spaces that represent space itself.

According to these teachings, the body is only the first layer.

Above the body are the five senses through which we experience the world.
Above the senses is the mind, where thoughts and emotions arise.
Above the mind is the intellect, which helps us reason and make decisions.
And above all of these is something deeper—the soul.

If you could close your eyes for a few seconds, it might help illustrate this.

With your eyes closed, your sense of sight becomes quiet, but your sense of hearing is still active. You can still hear my voice.

So the question becomes: who is the one inside that is listening right now?

That silent observer within—the one aware of the senses, the mind, and the body—is what the scriptures describe as the soul.

And that leads to a deeper question:

What exactly is a soul?

Let me share a simple story about a wave.

Once, a small wave was born in the vast Pacific Ocean. As it rose and moved along, it made friends with other waves. It played with them, learned with them, and grew alongside them—almost like going to a wave school.

As the wave grew, it began to notice other waves around it. Some were big and powerful, and it admired them. It dreamed of becoming one of those great waves someday. Other waves were small and weak, and sometimes it looked down on them.

One day, the wave noticed something frightening. Ahead of it was the shore, where waves were crashing and disappearing. One after another, they seemed to die.

The little wave became terrified.

Just then, a wise wave nearby spoke to him.
“Why are you afraid?” the wise wave asked.

“Don’t you see?” the little wave replied. “All those waves are crashing and disappearing. Soon that will happen to me too.”

The wise wave smiled gently and said, “You are looking at it the wrong way. When a wave crashes, it doesn’t really die. It only stops being a wave. But it continues to exist as water—which is what it truly is.”

In the same way, many spiritual traditions teach that we are not just the temporary forms we appear to be. Beneath our changing bodies and identities, we are like drops from a vast ocean.

An ocean of divine consciousness… an ocean of bliss.

Now you might wonder: which God am I talking about? After all, in India there are many gods, and around the world there are many different religions.

Think about the sun. In America we call it the **sun**. In Mexico, it is called **sol**. In India, it is called **surya**. But when the sun rises in America, we don’t call it an American sun. And when it rises in Mexico, we don’t call it a Mexican sun.

It is the same sun—only the names are different. If we didn’t know the languages or cultures, we might not even realize that the same reality is being described in different ways.

In a similar way, many spiritual traditions suggest that there is one ultimate reality, though people may call it by different names.

This brings us to an interesting question: do we **have** a soul, or are we **a** soul?

When someone dies, we often say, “He is gone,” or “She has left.” But the body is still there.

So who exactly left?

Let me offer another perspective.

This is a shirt. I say, *this is my shirt.*
I may say, *this is my car.*
Or, *this is my house.*

In each case, there is a clear distinction between **me** and **what belongs to me**.

In the same way, we often say, *this is my body.*

But if we think about it carefully, just as I am not my shirt, not my car, and not my house—perhaps I am not my body either.

There are many ways to understand the phrase **“Know thyself.”**

It may mean understanding our limits as human beings. We cannot control hurricanes or earthquakes.
It may mean understanding our beliefs and values—how they were formed and why we hold them.
Or it may mean discovering something deeper than our job titles, social labels, or achievements.

One insight I have come to appreciate is this: perhaps we are not merely bodies that have a soul, but souls that temporarily have a body.

Just as a person changes clothes, some traditions suggest that the soul changes bodies.

And with that thought, I invite you to reflect on the ancient words carved at Delphi.

I encourage you to explore what it means to **know thyself.**
